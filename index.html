What is skin conductance?

Your palms and fingertips are packed with sweat glands. They are wired to your sympathetic nervous system, the part of your nervous system behind the “fight or flight” reaction.

The body releases sweat during fight-or-flight as part of its preparation for intense physical action and thermal regulation.

When something grabs your attention, surprises you or stresses you, your brain sends a quick signal to these glands. They push a tiny amount of sweat up into their ducts.

Sweat is salty water, and salty water conducts electricity. The sensors on your fingers pass a very small current through your skin, far too small to feel, and measure how easily it flows. That is your skin conductance, measured in microsiemens (µS).

A tap-through explanation page follows the breath, the noise and the maths, so the clock keeps running while people read.

What to look for

A phasic rise starting 1–3 s after each marker, peaking 2–5 s later and recovering over 10–20 s. The deep breath and the noise give the sharpest, most reliable peaks. The maths announcement gives a peak at the marker, often followed by a slower climb during the countdown as anticipation builds. Marker times are listed at the end of the run.

Getting good signals

Let electrodes sit for a few minutes before starting. Cold hands give flat traces, so warm them first. A small share of people barely respond at all. If someone is flat, try the deep breath again on its own.

The noise

Half a second of white noise at full phone volume, with a white screen flash. It is startling but not harmful at phone-speaker level. The participant page never mentions it, so skip it for anyone who would rather not be startled.

    Why measure it?

    Skin conductance shows how strongly the body reacts to something, without relying on what people say. Researchers use it to ask questions like:

    • What grabs our attention? Responses are bigger to things that are new, surprising or personally important, and get smaller as something becomes familiar.
    • What do we fear? People with a strong fear of spiders respond more to pictures of spiders than to other pictures (Öhman & Soares, 1994).
    • What can it tell us about mental health? It allows researchers to investigate how conditions such as anxiety and PTSD might affect the way we learn and unlearn fear. For example, if a harmless signal is repeatedly paired with something unpleasant, the skin will eventually start to respond to the signal on its own. Normally, this response gradually fades when the signal is repeatedly presented without the unpleasant event. However, one study found that people with PTSD continued to show a response to the signal, whereas trauma-exposed people without PTSD did not (Orr et al., 2000).
